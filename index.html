Using Goats to Control Invasive Woody Plants in Kentucky Pastures

Goats feeding on cut woody browse. Photo: Michigan Technological University, 2024.

Introduction

Multiflora rose, autumn olive, and bush honeysuckle take up grazing space and keep returning after a single cutting. Targeted goat browsing gives Kentucky producers a practical way to weaken that brush over time.

Why this works

Goats naturally eat leaves, twigs, and brush before grass. A Kentucky farmer can use that habit to clear pasture and still sell the goats for meat later (Launchbaugh & Walker, 2006).

The work still has to be managed. Choose the problem area, keep the goats there long enough to strip most of the leaves, then move them before useful forage is overgrazed. When the brush sends up new growth, bring the herd back. In a 2022 multiflora rose study, one season of browsing left stems 35% shorter with a 56% lower leaf-to-stem mass ratio (Bowden et al., 2022).

What this site covers

This site introduces the three main invasive shrubs, explains why goats suit the job, lays out a field plan, covers fencing and goat care, and lists Kentucky resources for local advice. Spring to early summer is the best window for heavy browsing (Treadwell et al., 2023). Plan on repeating for two to three seasons, since woody plants resprout after a single pass (Natural Resources Conservation Service, 2010).

Know the three main invaders

These shrubs are easy to miss when there are only a few. Once they form a thicket, they take sunlight, space, and water away from useful forage and make it harder for livestock to move through the field.

Do not rely on one feature alone. Plant identification can be tricky. If you are uncertain, take clear photos of the leaves, stem, flowers or berries and ask your county Extension agent before you make a control plan.

Section 2

Why goats can do the job

A mower cuts everything at one height. Goats work around rocks and rough ground, reach into thickets, strip leaves, and may debark young stems. Spanish, Kiko, Nigerian Dwarf, and Boer goats can all be used for brush control.

What the research shows

  • Thorns are not much of a barrier. Goats use their mobile lips to pick leaves from prickly stems and can tolerate tannins found in woody plants (Launchbaugh & Walker, 2006).
  • They reach higher than a mower. Standing on their hind legs lets goats browse stems up to about five feet high (Natural Resources Conservation Service, 2010).
  • They can slow new seedlings. Immature seeds eaten by goats rarely survive digestion (Launchbaugh & Walker, 2006).
  • Repeated browsing changes the plant. Bowden and colleagues found 35% shorter multiflora rose stems and a 56% lower leaf-to-stem mass ratio after one season (Bowden et al., 2022).
  • Heavy pressure can open a thicket. A North Carolina study reduced multiflora rose canopy from 12 square meters to 2.5 square meters and eliminated the canes in the treated area (Luginbuhl et al., 2001).
  1. First passBrowse hard when leaves are well developed in spring.
  2. Rest the paddockMove goats so the useful forage can recover.
  3. Return to regrowthCome back before the shrub rebuilds a full canopy.
  4. Repeat for several yearsPlan on at least two to three growing seasons for lasting change.

How the main control options compare

These figures are planning estimates from the research (Treadwell et al., 2023). Actual costs will change with acreage, terrain, labor, equipment, and the density of the brush.

Section 3

Build a workable field plan

Start with one paddock you can watch closely. A smaller trial will teach you more than putting the whole farm into the first attempt.

  1. Walk the site.Mark the target brush, steep ground, water, shade, gates, and any plants you do not want eaten.
  2. Choose a starting stock rate.About 3–4 goats per acre can be a starting point for dense brush. Intensive early-season work may use 8–12 goats per acre, but adjust to the paddock and animal condition (Natural Resources Conservation Service, 2010).
  3. Browse at the right time.Spring and early summer are the main window, when the plants are most vulnerable (Treadwell et al., 2023). Watch the brush and the goats, not just the calendar.
  4. Move, rest, and return.Take goats out before good forage is overgrazed. Come back for the fresh woody regrowth and repeat for at least two to three seasons (Treadwell et al., 2023).
  5. Keep notes.Photos from the same spot can show whether brush cover and access are improving from year to year.
If the goats have eaten the useful forage and are staring through the fence, the paddock was kept too long.

Section 4

Fencing and goat care

Targeted browsing only works when the goats stay safely contained and receive the same daily care they would in any other grazing system.

  1. Check for poisonous plants.Remove or fence off problem plants before goats enter. Never feed ornamental clippings.
  2. Set reliable fencing.Portable electric netting, a well-designed multi-strand electric fence, or woven wire with an offset electric strand can work (Treadwell et al., 2023).
  3. Train goats to the fence.Let the herd learn the fence before moving into a brushy paddock, and never electrify barbed wire (Treadwell et al., 2023; Natural Resources Conservation Service, 2010).
  4. Meet daily needs.Provide clean water, shade or shelter, and minerals formulated for goats. Watch body condition and parasites.
Good brush control should never hurt the goats or the good pasture.
Animal safety comes first. Hungry goats are more likely to sample toxic plants. Check the paddock before turnout and move the herd before useful forage is overgrazed.
